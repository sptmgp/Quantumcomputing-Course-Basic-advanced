1. Why this matters industrially

The split-operator technique (SOT) — the workhorse nobody outside physics has heard of

Split-operator techniques propagate a probability density through phase space exactly, using fast Fourier transforms. They are the standard tool for simulating quantum wavepackets, plasma kinetics, and classical chaotic systems. Their limitation is well known and unavoidable: the computational grid needed grows exponentially with the number of physical dimensions — the "curse of dimensionality." This is precisely why machine learning has been proposed as an alternative: a neural network doesn't need a grid at all.

The same mathematics runs three industries

Quantum computing (IBM). Every real quantum computer loses its delicate quantum state to its environment — a process described by the Lindblad master equation, the direct quantum generalization of the classical transport equation SOT solves. This isn't theoretical: IBM's own Qiskit Runtime uses a Pauli-Lindblad noise model as the core of its production error-mitigation stack, fitted to real hardware measurements and used to mathematically correct results from actual quantum chips.
Chip design (Intel, Samsung, and every major semiconductor manufacturer). How electrons move through a transistor is governed by the Boltzmann transport equation — mathematically the same family as the Liouville equation this page is about, just for charge carriers instead of a single particle. This is the physics underneath TCAD (Technology Computer-Aided Design), the simulation software every chip manufacturer depends on to design transistors before fabrication. Samsung Electronics has published research combining deep learning with TCAD specifically because full Boltzmann-equation simulation is too slow for production use; other published work reports machine-learning-accelerated TCAD achieving speedups of several orders of magnitude on convergence-limited cases. Physics-informed neural networks have already been applied directly to Boltzmann-transport-based device modeling in the research literature.
Large-scale AI-for-science efforts (Google and others). Beyond this specific equation family, major AI labs have made substantial, publicly known investments in using neural networks to accelerate physical simulation generally — weather, materials discovery, molecular dynamics. The specific question this repository asks — does a neural network trained to accelerate a physics simulation actually respect the physics, or just approximate the training signal — is a question that applies directly to that broader effort, not only to the one system tested here.

Why this is urgent for "the AI era" specifically

The moment a company replaces a slow, exact numerical method with a fast neural network, they inherit a new risk that the exact method never had: the network can be wrong in a way that looks right. A traditional numerical solver either converges or visibly fails. A trained neural network reports a smooth, low training loss regardless of whether it's quietly violating the physics — as this repository demonstrates concretely, not hypothetically. As AI-accelerated simulation moves from research demos toward production use in exactly these industries, knowing how to detect and prevent this specific failure mode stops being an academic curiosity.

2. This paper's objectives

  1. Test whether AI can genuinely replace a trusted physics solver — not in theory, but on a real, exactly-solvable benchmark system, so the answer is measurable rather than argued.
  2. Diagnose the mechanism when it fails, not just document that it does — find the specific reason a network can train "successfully" while violating a conservation law.
  3. Verify that diagnosis independently, on a different physical system (a quantum qubit) governed by the same mathematical structure, to confirm the explanation is general and not a one-off artifact.
  4. Build and mathematically prove a structural fix — an architecture where the conservation law is guaranteed by a theorem, not by hoping training went well.
  5. Honestly test that fix's actual limits — under real chaos, across random initialization, and under added complexity — rather than reporting only the best case.

4. How the PINN fails

A neural network can train "successfully" — smooth loss curve, low final error — while quietly violating a law of physics its own equation says it must obey.

The equation being solved

$$\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + p\,\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial q} - V'(q)\,\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial p} = 0$$

A physics-informed neural network (PINN) approximates the solution directly: a network ρ_θ(q,p,t) is trained so that plugging it into the equation above gives close to zero, using automatic differentiation for exact derivatives. This sounds reasonable. It is not sufficient.

The mechanism

The mass violation is present from the very first instant and barely changes over the whole simulated period — evidence it's a static defect, not accumulating error.

  1. The training domain is far larger than where the true answer lives (a tight blob vs. a huge box).
  2. A flat, low-amplitude background trivially satisfies the equation almost everywhere in that empty space.
  3. The optimizer has no incentive to remove it — it costs nothing against the loss.
  4. Integrated over the whole domain, that faint background is exactly the missing/excess mass.

5. How the HNN fixes it — by construction, not by training

Don't train a network to output a density that happens to conserve probability. Train it to learn something whose structure makes conservation mathematically unavoidable — before training even starts.

What the network learns instead

$$\dot q_{\text{pred}} = \frac{\partial H_\theta}{\partial p}, \qquad \dot p_{\text{pred}} = -\frac{\partial H_\theta}{\partial q}$$

A Hamiltonian Neural Network (HNN) learns a scalar function Hθ(q,p) from local motion data, then a density is propagated by moving points along this learned flow — the same method of characteristics used for the exact SOT reference above, just with a learned right-hand side.

The proof

Claim: for any function Hθ(q,p), however inaccurate, the vector field above is exactly divergence-free.

Proof: $$\nabla\cdot(\dot q,\dot p) = \frac{\partial^2 H_\theta}{\partial q\partial p} - \frac{\partial^2 H_\theta}{\partial p\partial q} = 0$$ by equality of mixed partial derivatives (Clairaut's theorem) — true for any twice-differentiable function, trained well or badly.

Zero divergence means the flow cannot compress or expand phase-space volume — Liouville's theorem. Mass conservation isn't something this network has to learn. It's structurally incapable of violating it.

In the HNN training animation below, reported mass reads 1.000 at epoch 0 — before any training. What improves over training is only the shape. That's the theorem working, not the optimizer.

Where it still struggles

Extended to two coupled oscillators: the linear dynamics is learned almost exactly; the nonlinear coupling between the two nodes is not.

Across 5 random seeds, chaos-tracking accuracy varied by up to 38× — training loss alone did not predict which seed would perform best.

7. Honest limitations

  • Low-dimensional toy problem. 2D classical phase space, one qubit, one small 4D network — the higher-dimensional regime motivating AI-based solvers in the first place is not yet tested.
  • HNN training data came from the true equations of motion. A real application without that luxury — learning from noisy observations instead — is untested here.
  • Single-run point estimates are unreliable. The 5-seed test showed up to 38× spread; report a distribution, not one run, if you deploy this.
  • The chaos test only covers 4 kick periods. Whether the HNN's advantage plateaus, keeps eroding, or is eventually overtaken at longer horizons is unknown.
  • This is a preprint-stage draft, not peer-reviewed work. Real authorship, independent expert review, and an AI-assistance disclosure are still outstanding.
